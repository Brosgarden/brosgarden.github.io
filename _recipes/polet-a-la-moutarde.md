A classic French-inspired chicken braise featuring deeply browned, bone-in chicken thighs and drumsticks in a silky Dijon mustard sauce. Homemade chicken stock provides the foundation, while white wine, shallots, cream, and fresh tarragon contribute acidity, richness, and aromatic complexity.

The sauce is built through careful reduction rather than a roux. Dijon mustard is incorporated into the braising liquid for depth, while whole-grain mustard and fresh tarragon are added at the end to preserve their sharper flavors. A small amount of cold butter finishes the sauce with a glossy texture.

Active preparation is approximately 20–25 minutes, with an optional 4–12-hour dry brine. Allow approximately 75 minutes for browning, reduction, braising, and sauce finishing, or roughly 90–100 minutes total. Actual braising time depends on the size of the chicken pieces and the temperature of the cooking liquid.

## Ingredients

### Mustard-Braised Chicken

- 1,400 g (approximately 3 lb) bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs and drumsticks
- 14 g kosher salt, adjusted for the salinity of the stock
- 2 g freshly ground black pepper
- 15 g neutral oil
- 100 g shallots, finely diced
- 12 g garlic, finely minced
- 180 g dry white wine, such as Sauvignon Blanc or unoaked Chardonnay
- 350 g homemade chicken stock, preferably unsalted
- 45 g Dijon mustard
- 3 sprigs fresh thyme

### Mustard-Tarragon Cream Sauce

- 80 g heavy cream
- 15 g whole-grain mustard
- 6–8 g fresh tarragon leaves, finely chopped
- 20 g cold unsalted butter, cut into small cubes
- 3–5 g fresh lemon juice or white wine vinegar, optional
- Kosher salt and freshly ground black pepper, to taste

## Equipment

- 3-quart All-Clad sauté pan or similarly sized oven-safe braising vessel, provided the chicken fits without crowding
- Instant-read thermometer
- Fine-mesh strainer, optional
- Breville Control Freak, optional for stovetop braising and sauce finishing

## Directions

### 1. Dry-brine the chicken

Pat the chicken thoroughly dry with paper towels.

Season evenly with 14 g kosher salt and 2 g black pepper. If the homemade chicken stock is already salted, reduce the seasoning accordingly.

Arrange the chicken skin-side up on a wire rack over a tray and refrigerate uncovered for 4–12 hours, if time allows. This improves seasoning penetration and helps dry the skin for more effective browning.

If cooking immediately, season the chicken and let it stand while preparing the remaining ingredients.

Remove the chicken from the refrigerator approximately 15–20 minutes before browning. Pat the skin dry again if necessary.

### 2. Brown the chicken

Preheat the oven to 325°F, conventional.

Heat the sauté pan over medium heat. Add 15 g neutral oil.

Place the chicken skin-side down in the pan, leaving enough space between pieces for moisture to evaporate. Brown in batches if necessary.

Cook skin-side down for approximately 8–12 minutes, adjusting the heat to maintain steady browning without scorching. The skin should develop a deep golden-brown color and release relatively easily from the pan.

Turn the chicken and brown the opposite side for another 3–4 minutes.

Transfer the chicken to a plate, skin-side up.

The chicken does not need to be cooked through at this stage. The objective is to render some of the fat, develop color, and establish a flavorful fond for the sauce.

Pour off excess rendered fat, leaving approximately 15–20 g in the pan.

### 3. Sweat the aromatics

Reduce the heat to medium-low.

Add the diced shallots to the rendered chicken fat. Cook for approximately 3–4 minutes, stirring frequently, until softened and translucent.

Avoid significant browning; the shallots should contribute sweetness without introducing bitterness.

Add the minced garlic and cook for another 30–45 seconds, just until fragrant.

If the fond begins to darken excessively, add a small splash of the measured white wine to arrest browning.

### 4. Deglaze and reduce the wine

Add the remaining white wine, scraping the bottom of the pan thoroughly with a wooden spoon to dissolve the accumulated fond.

Increase the heat to medium and bring the wine to a steady simmer.

Reduce by approximately two-thirds, from 180 g to roughly 60 g.

This generally takes 5–8 minutes, depending on the pan diameter and heat intensity.

The reduced wine should smell aromatic rather than sharply alcoholic. Its acidity will provide balance against the mustard, cream, and gelatin-rich stock.

Avoid reducing completely dry, which can scorch the shallots and fond.

### 5. Build the mustard braising liquid

Add 350 g homemade chicken stock and 45 g Dijon mustard to the reduced wine.

Whisk thoroughly to disperse the mustard evenly throughout the liquid.

Add the thyme sprigs and bring the mixture to a gentle simmer.

Return the chicken to the pan, skin-side up, along with any accumulated juices.

Arrange the pieces in a single layer. Ideally, the liquid should reach approximately one-third to halfway up the sides of the chicken, leaving the skin exposed.

The exact liquid level depends on the shape of the pan and the size of the chicken pieces. If necessary, add a small amount of additional stock.

Do not completely submerge the chicken.

### 6. Braise the chicken

Transfer the pan to the preheated 325°F oven, partially covered with an oven-safe lid.

Braise for approximately 35–50 minutes, beginning to check after 30 minutes.

The braising liquid should maintain a gentle simmer rather than a vigorous boil.

Check the thickest portion of the chicken with an instant-read thermometer, avoiding contact with the bone.

Target an internal temperature of approximately 180–190°F for tender dark meat.

Temperature is a useful guide, but tenderness is the final criterion. A skewer should enter the thickest portion with little resistance, and the meat should be tender without completely falling apart.

If the liquid reduces excessively during cooking, add 30–60 g additional stock.

Once tender, remove the chicken to a warm plate and tent loosely with foil.

**Control Freak alternative:** Instead of transferring the pan to the oven, use probe control with the probe immersed in the braising liquid, not touching the pan or chicken. Set the liquid target to 190°F with Slow intensity and partially cover the pan. Expect approximately 50–75 minutes, checking the chicken for tenderness. Keep the probe submerged throughout cooking.

### 7. Reduce the braising liquid

Remove and discard the thyme sprigs.

Skim any excessive surface fat from the braising liquid, leaving a small amount for flavor.

For a particularly smooth sauce, strain the liquid through a fine-mesh strainer into a small saucepan, pressing gently on the shallots to extract their flavor. Alternatively, leave the shallots in the sauce for a more rustic presentation.

Bring the liquid to a moderate simmer and reduce until concentrated and beginning to develop a lightly syrupy consistency.

Depending on evaporation during braising, this may take approximately 5–10 minutes.

Aim for roughly 200–250 g of concentrated braising liquid before incorporating the cream, although the correct endpoint is determined by flavor and consistency rather than an exact weight.

Taste periodically. The liquid should have a pronounced savory character with balanced mustard and wine acidity.

Do not reduce aggressively if the stock is already salty.

### 8. Finish the mustard-tarragon cream sauce

Reduce the heat to low.

Add 80 g heavy cream and stir until fully incorporated.

Simmer gently for approximately 2–3 minutes, allowing the cream to integrate and the sauce to thicken slightly.

Add 15 g whole-grain mustard and stir to combine.

Remove the pan from direct heat.

Add 6–8 g finely chopped fresh tarragon and stir gently.

Whisk in the 20 g cold butter, a few cubes at a time, allowing each addition to emulsify before adding the next.

The finished sauce should be smooth, glossy, and sufficiently thick to coat the back of a spoon while still flowing easily.

Taste and adjust the seasoning.

If the sauce tastes excessively rich or flat, add 3 g lemon juice or white wine vinegar, taste, and increase to 5 g if necessary. The wine and mustard may already provide sufficient acidity.

Add additional salt or black pepper only after evaluating the finished sauce.

Avoid vigorous boiling after incorporating the butter, which can compromise the emulsion.

**Control Freak option:** Use probe control with a target of 175–180°F and Slow intensity while incorporating the cream. Remove the pan from heat before mounting with cold butter.

### 9. Finish the chicken and serve

For the best skin texture, place the cooked chicken skin-side up on a small baking sheet.

Broil briefly, approximately 2–4 minutes, until the skin regains some crispness. Watch closely, as the skin can brown rapidly.

Alternatively, omit the broiler step for a more traditional, softly braised presentation.

Spoon the finished mustard-tarragon sauce onto warmed plates or a serving platter.

Arrange the chicken over the sauce, keeping most of the skin exposed.

Spoon a little additional sauce around the chicken rather than directly over the skin.

Garnish with a small amount of fresh tarragon and freshly ground black pepper.

Serve immediately.

## Suggested Accompaniments

### Roasted Potatoes

Roasted Yukon Gold or russet potatoes provide a crisp contrast to the tender chicken and creamy mustard sauce.

For approximately four servings:

- 700–800 g potatoes, cut into evenly sized pieces
- 20 g neutral oil or olive oil
- 7–8 g kosher salt
- Freshly ground black pepper
- Fresh thyme, optional

Roast at 425°F convection until golden and tender, approximately 30–40 minutes, turning once.

If using the same oven for the chicken, prepare the potatoes while the chicken braises and roast them at the higher temperature while the chicken rests and the sauce is finished.

### Haricots Verts

Blanch approximately 350–400 g trimmed green beans in well-salted boiling water until crisp-tender, approximately 2–4 minutes depending on thickness.

Drain thoroughly and refresh briefly in cold water if not serving immediately.

Just before serving, sauté in 15 g butter until heated through. Finish with a little lemon zest, salt, and black pepper.

The beans should remain bright and lightly crisp, providing contrast to the richness of the sauce.

## Notes

- **Chicken selection:** Bone-in, skin-on thighs are particularly well suited to this preparation. Drumsticks also work well, although individual pieces may require slightly different cooking times. Boneless thighs will cook considerably faster and should be checked earlier.
- **Stock quality:** A gelatin-rich homemade chicken stock is ideal. Its natural gelatin gives the finished sauce body and gloss without requiring flour or a roux.
- **Stock salinity:** The recipe assumes unsalted or lightly salted stock. Because the braising liquid is reduced, heavily seasoned stock can produce an excessively salty sauce.
- **Dijon mustard:** Incorporating Dijon into the braising liquid creates a rounded mustard flavor. For a sharper finish, reserve 10–15 g of the measured Dijon and incorporate it alongside the whole-grain mustard at the end.
- **Whole-grain mustard:** Adding it after reduction preserves its texture and more pronounced mustard character.
- **Tarragon:** Fresh tarragon is strongly preferred. Add it near the end to preserve its delicate anise-like aroma. Dried tarragon is more muted and should be used sparingly.
- **Cream:** The 80 g quantity is intentionally restrained. The sauce should remain stock-forward and mustard-forward rather than resembling a heavy cream gravy.
- **Wine:** A dry, unoaked white wine is preferable. Avoid sweet wines or heavily oaked Chardonnay, which can overwhelm the mustard and tarragon.
- **Sauce reduction:** Reduce the stock-based braising liquid before adding cream. This concentrates savory flavor without exposing the cream to prolonged cooking.
- **Butter mounting:** Cold butter added off heat produces a glossy, lightly thickened sauce. Avoid boiling after the butter is incorporated.
- **Skin texture:** The initial browning develops flavor, but braising naturally softens the skin. A brief broiler finish improves texture, although it is optional.
- **Internal temperature:** Chicken is conventionally considered safely cooked at 165°F, but dark meat generally develops a more tender texture at 180–190°F. Judge final doneness by both temperature and tenderness.
- **Pan capacity:** The chicken must fit comfortably in a single layer. A 3-quart sauté pan may be too small for 1.4 kg of bone-in pieces, depending on their size. Use a larger braising vessel if necessary.
- **Make-ahead:** The chicken can be braised one day ahead and refrigerated in its cooking liquid. Reheat gently, then reduce and finish the sauce with cream, mustard, tarragon, and butter immediately before serving.
- **Scaling:** Scale the chicken, aromatics, and seasoning proportionally, but adjust stock volume according to the pan's dimensions. Maintaining the correct liquid depth is more important than strictly scaling the stock quantity.
